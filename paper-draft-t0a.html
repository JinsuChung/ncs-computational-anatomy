The Computational Anatomy of a National Competency Standard: Regularity, Redundancy, and Terminological Fragmentation in Korea's NCS

Jinsu Chunga, Han-Cheol Ryua,* — aDept. of AI Convergence, Sahmyook University, Seoul, Republic of Korea. *Corresponding author.

Highlights

  • Template deviations are local: half of 859 sit in 26 of 1,109 occupations
  • Rules merge 8.2% of surface forms; embeddings propose 28.1%, 19.2–23.6% verified
  • Experts split on one boundary: "same" 28–48%, "same or broader" 62–66%
  • ESCO replication reproduces the rule ceiling, the knee and the chaining failure
  • Downweighting generic concepts, not the merge cap, governs occupation similarity

Abstract

National competency standards increasingly feed skill taxonomies, knowledge graphs, and LLM-assisted systems, yet the properties deciding how their text can be processed are rarely measured first. We report a complete census of Korea's National Competency Standards (1,109 occupations; 196,658 performance criteria; 2.46 million knowledge/skill/attitude items). Exploratory aggregates and confirmatory tests are kept apart: hypotheses were registered before occupation-level testing and labelled by whether aggregates had been seen, with split-half replication and false-discovery control. The syntactic residue rules cannot parse is small and enumerable: 99.56% of criteria follow the mandated template, half the deviations in 26 occupations. Redundancy is an industry property, not a size artifact. Terminological fragmentation resists rules: normalization merges 8.2% of surface forms while embedding alignment proposes 28.1%, of which an estimated 19.2–23.6% survives fourteen external raters under a conservative mergeability definition; cos ≥ .90 is the precision knee, transitive co-membership weak. Experts disagree on one boundary only: individually they span 28–48% on “same concept” but 62–66% once hierarchical pairs count. Shared vocabulary recovers the official taxonomy only partially. ESCO, where alternative labels give exact ground truth, reproduces the rule ceiling, the knee, the transitive precision and the chaining failure but not the template; a second encoder reproduces every aggregate. Downstream, query-time expansion by direct neighbours beats every persisted concept layer on ESCO; on NCS, generic-concept downweighting, not the merge policy, governs similarity, and alignment's whole gain comes from linking single-occupation expressions across occupations; within-occupation merges add nothing. We release the alias dictionary, candidate merges, metrics, and code.

Keywords: competency standards; corpus profiling; terminology normalization; embedding alignment; knowledge graph construction; open government data

1. Introduction

Governments have spent decades writing down what work requires. Korea's National Competency Standards (NCS) describe 1,109 occupations through nearly 200,000 "can-do" performance criteria and 2.46 million knowledge, skill, and attitude items; the European ESCO, the U.S. O*NET, and comparable frameworks elsewhere play similar roles. These corpora are now routinely treated as inputs: to skill taxonomies, to knowledge graphs, and — since large language models made extraction cheap — to automated ontology construction [1, 2, 10, 11].

What is rarely done first is to measure the material. A pipeline designer needs to know, before choosing tools, how regular the text is (can rules parse it?), how redundant it is (how large is the real work?), how badly one concept is split across spellings (what must alignment repair?), and how much structure the corpus already encodes independently of its official taxonomy. Without those numbers, the choice between deterministic rules and learned models is made by fashion rather than by evidence, and the cost of a full-scale build cannot be estimated at all.

We call the missing step computational profiling: measuring, before any construction, the properties of a standard's text that decide where rules end, where a model is needed, and where a person must adjudicate. The framework has four measurements — regularity, redundancy, fragmentation, and latent structure — and one output, a division of labour between rules, models, and human judgment that is stated in numbers rather than chosen by preference. This paper applies it to Korea's NCS as a complete census, checks which of its findings transfer to ESCO, and shows that the numbers change decisions downstream. Its principal contribution is the third measurement and what surrounds it: how badly one concept is split across surface forms, how much of that split rules and embeddings each repair, and how human judges behave at the boundary where "same concept" has to be decided. The other measurements set that result up (RQ1, RQ2), corroborate it structurally (RQ4), or describe the corpus for completeness (RQ5, reported in Appendix A). We ask:

RQ1. How syntactically regular are performance criteria, and where does the residue live?
RQ2. How redundant is the KSA layer, and is redundancy an artifact of corpus size or a property of industries?
RQ3. How severely is a single concept fragmented across surface forms, and how much of that fragmentation can rule-based normalization repair versus embedding alignment?
RQ4. How much of the official occupational taxonomy is recoverable from shared KSA vocabulary alone?
RQ5. How are revision recency, competency level, and digital vocabulary distributed across industries?

Contributions, in order of weight:

(1) The framework and its first complete application. A computational profile of a national competency standard along four axes, run as a census with the boundary between exploratory aggregates and confirmatory tests managed explicitly (§3.2): hypotheses registered before occupation-level testing, labelled by whether they were formed before or after exploratory aggregates were seen, with split-half replication and FDR control. (2) The rule/model boundary for this corpus type, in numbers. The syntactic residue that escapes rules is small and enumerable (859 criteria, half in 26 occupations), but rules repair only 8.2% of terminological fragmentation while embeddings propose 28.1%, of which an estimated 19.2–23.6% survives human validation under the study's conservative mergeability definition — a quantified division of labour rather than a methodological preference. On ESCO, with an exact ground truth, the rule ceiling, the .90 knee, the ~30% transitive precision and the chaining failure reproduce; the syntactic template does not. (3) Where human judgment divides. Among four domain experts judging the same pairs, agreement on whether two terms are related at all is near-identical (62.0–66.0%) while agreement on whether they are the same concept is not (28.0–48.0%): the disagreement an adjudication stage must absorb is concentrated at one boundary — a result a two-rater ceiling cannot expose. (4) A measured methodological caution. Naive transitive closure over an embedding similarity graph merges 5.2% of all keys into a single component at cos ≥ .90 (29.9% at cos ≥ .85); modularity-based clustering removes the chain while leaving aggregate recovery unchanged (28.2% → 28.1%), so the aggregate statistic cannot detect the failure. (5) Evidence that the measurements change decisions, in two corpora: downstream similarity is governed by downweighting generic concepts; query-time expansion by direct edges beats every persisted community policy; the downstream value of the embedding stage lies in linking single-occupation expressions across occupations; and the community-size cap governs none of the three tasks we measured but sets the precision of what is written into a published concept layer. (6) Released artifacts: an alias dictionary (19,881 rule-stage entries); a separate candidate file of the embedding stage at cap ≤ 5 (27,721 groups covering 65,557 keys), each group carrying its size, direct-edge share, the expected precision implied by §4.6, and the human verdicts where a member pair was judged; per-occupation metrics for all 1,109 occupations, a maintainer-facing audit list (typographical variants, template-deviant occupations, revision-year dispersion), and the analysis code.

Perspective and scope. This paper looks at NCS from one side only: that of someone about to build a knowledge resource from it. Every question above is a question about the standard as input — what rules can parse, how much work deduplication removes, how much alignment must repair, how much structure is already latent. We measure the text; we do not evaluate the standard. In particular we make no claims about the labour market it describes, about the committees that author it, or about what it ought to contain; where a result touches those questions (§4.5, §5), we report the distribution and stop. The choice is deliberate: a single, explicit vantage point is what makes the numbers usable as design rules. Readings of the same corpus as a policy instrument or as a genre of institutional writing are worthwhile but need evidence this paper does not collect.

2. Related Work

2.1 Competency standards as data

National and international competency frameworks differ less in ambition than in data model. O*NET was designed from the outset as a database of quantitative descriptors over occupations [1]; ESCO is distributed as an RDF/SKOS ontology whose occupations, skills, and qualifications carry declared relations across 27 languages [2]; sector frameworks such as SFIA maintain versioned skill hierarchies with level descriptors. NCS occupies a different point in this space: its sentence-level content — nearly 200,000 performance criteria and 2.46 million KSA items — is denser than any of these, but beyond the classification tree it declares no relations, and the knowledge it encodes is reachable only by reading. Korean scholarship has treated NCS as a policy instrument, as a curriculum input, or as a target label set, but not as a corpus. Representative lines are reviews of its development and of its integration with the national qualification framework [23]; an ontology hand-built for the competency units of a single occupation [24]; network text analysis of NCS application case reports rather than of the standard's own text [25]; and, closest to the present work, automatic mapping of job postings onto NCS categories, evaluated on 284 postings against seven sub-categories with sentence embeddings and a large language model [26]. That last line does use NCS computationally, but only as a label inventory: the text being measured is the postings', not the standard's. The same holds for the adjacent Korean work on skill extraction, where a domain-adapted language model trained on IT job advertisements extracts skills and classifies tasks against a 59-category taxonomy [27]: again the labour-market text is the object of measurement, and the standard is at most a reference vocabulary. A complete-census computational profile of the standard's own text — its regularity, its redundancy, and the fragmentation of its concepts — has not, to our knowledge, been published.

2.2 Corpus profiling before construction

The case for documenting a dataset before using it is now well established — datasheets [3] and data statements [4] ask creators to record provenance, composition, and intended use. We specialise that norm in one direction: rather than describing where a corpus came from, we measure the properties that determine how it can be processed. Regularity, redundancy, and fragmentation are not provenance facts; they are the quantities that decide whether a rule, a model, or a person is the right tool for each layer, and they can be measured before any tool is built.

2.3 Terminology extraction and normalization

Term extraction from specialised text has a long lineage in frequency- and unithood-based methods such as C-value [5]; the redundancy we report in §4.2 is precisely the property those methods exploit, and the fragmentation we report in §4.3 is the property they leave unresolved. Joining variant surface forms of one concept is an entity-resolution problem [6], and our two-stage design — a deterministic normalization key followed by embedding-neighbour search above a threshold — is a blocking-then-matching pipeline in the sense of [7]. The chaining artefact of §4.3 is the familiar failure mode of single-linkage merging in record linkage, here observed at corpus scale and quantified against human judgment.

2.4 Ontology learning with LLMs

Learning ontological structure from text predates large language models — Text2Onto [8] and the survey of Wong et al. [9] map the classical pipeline of term, concept, taxonomy, and relation extraction — and has been re-energised by them: LLMs4OL [10] benchmarks language models on those subtasks, and Pan et al. [11] chart the design space for combining language models with knowledge graphs. This paper sits before that pipeline rather than inside it. We do not compete on extraction accuracy; we measure the size and character of the candidate space an extractor will face, and the precision of the cheapest tools that reduce it, so that the expensive stages — model-based extraction and human or model adjudication — are sized by evidence.

2.5 Computational profiling as a framework

The four measurements of §1 are not new individually; what is new is running them together, before construction, as the basis for assigning work to rules, models, and people. Each has a home literature. Regularity and redundancy are fitness-for-use questions in the data-quality sense of Wang and Strong [28] and Pipino et al. [29]: a text's quality is not intrinsic but relative to the processing it is about to receive, and profiling is the act of measuring that fitness. Fragmentation is the entity-resolution problem [6, 7] seen from the vocabulary side, and its resolution is the ontology alignment problem of Euzenat and Shvaiko [30] restricted to one corpus; the concept layer such a resolution produces is what SKOS-style vocabularies [31] and thesaurus standards [32] exist to hold, and their notions of preferred and alternative labels are the reason ESCO can serve as an exact reference in §4.7. The human validation of §4.6 belongs to human-in-the-loop entity resolution, where systems such as Magellan [33] and CrowdER [34] treat the adjudication budget as a design variable — precisely the quantity our precision-by-band and inter-rater results are meant to size. The chaining artefact of §4.3 is the single-linkage pathology long documented in cluster analysis [35], and the distinction that organises §4.9 — between an irreversible canonicalisation written into a resource and a reversible query-time expansion — is the distinction the query-expansion literature draws between index-time and query-time term substitution [36]. Read together, these literatures already contain every operation this paper performs; what they do not contain is a measured account of what happens when they are applied, in sequence, to a national standard. That account is the contribution, and the framework is its name.

3. Data and Method

3.1 Corpus

We use the complete NCS release of February 2026 (the "NCS Information Network DB, by major category" export dated 2026-02, downloaded 5 June 2026) as loaded into a relational store: 24 major categories, 81 middle, 274 minor, and 1,109 detailed categories (occupations); 13,435 competency units carrying levels L1–L8; 47,620 unit elements; 196,658 performance criteria; and 2,457,556 KSA items, of which 355,896 are distinct strings. A separate national framework of generic employability skills (7 areas, 21 sub-abilities, 63 elements) is loaded but used only for context here.

Competency unit codes carry a suffix of the form _YYvN. Korean documentation defines this field as the development/revision year and the version as the ordinal of development. We verified this internally: mean year rises monotonically with version (v1 2017.2, v2 2018.4, v3 2020.7, v4 2021.9, v5 2022.5, v6 2023.1, v7 2023.3; r = .470, n = 13,435). Were the year fixed at first development, units with more revisions would carry older years, so the observed pattern is inconsistent with that alternative; the year distribution is also spread evenly over 2013–2025, ruling out a one-off code reissue. We therefore read the field as revision recency, while noting it records when a unit was last touched, not how much changed.

3.2 Pre-registration and inference

Exploratory statistics at the major-category level were computed first and are logged as such (log EA11 of 9 September 2026, released with the code as scripts/ncs_corpus_stats_ea11.sql and its output in the repository). Hypotheses formed after seeing those aggregates are labelled PEEK; hypotheses about quantities not computed in exploration are labelled CLEAN, and both labels appear in the results tables. To preserve inferential information, all confirmatory tests are run at a resolution exploration did not see — the occupation (n = 1,109) — since within-major variance was unobserved. Each test is additionally run on two halves formed by seeded random splitting of occupations within each major category, and reported as replicating only when both halves agree in sign and significance. The nine primary p-values are corrected together with Benjamini–Hochberg at q = .05. Because n = 1,109 makes trivial differences significant, effect sizes (ε², Cramér's V, ρ, Kendall's W) are reported as the primary quantities.

What the statistics refer to. The corpus is a census of one release, so three kinds of number appear and should not be read alike. Census quantities (the 99.56% template rate, the 8.2% rule recovery, the 28.1% candidate recovery) describe the February 2026 release exactly and carry no interval. Structural tests (Kruskal–Wallis, Spearman, the permutation null for NMI, split-half replication) do not estimate a super-population; they ask whether a pattern across occupations survives random partition and chance composition, and their p-values and effect sizes are read in that sense. Sampled estimates (merge precision, verified recovery, inter-rater α) rest on judged samples and carry bootstrap intervals over items. Generalisation beyond the release is argued separately, by replication on ESCO (§4.7) and a second encoder (§4.8), not by inference from the census.

3.3 Measures

Template compliance is the proportion of performance criteria whose text ends in the canonical "~할 수 있다 ('is able to …')" form, allowing whitespace and terminal-period variants; deviations are typed by their ending. Uniqueness rate is distinct KSA strings over KSA items within an occupation. Fragmentation is measured in two stages: a deterministic normalization key (NFKC, removal of role suffixes such as "지식/기술/능력/방법 ('knowledge / skill / ability / method')", removal of whitespace and punctuation, case folding), then an embedding stage described in §3.4. Occupation similarity is Jaccard overlap of normalization-key sets, over keys appearing in at least two occupations. Digital vocabulary is the share of KSA items matching a pre-declared lexicon (A: 디지털 ('digital'), 데이터 ('data'), 인공지능 ('artificial intelligence'), AI, 소프트웨어 ('software'), 자동화 ('automation'), 프로그램 ('program(me)'), 전산 ('computing'); B adds IoT, 빅데이터 ('big data'), 클라우드 ('cloud'), 정보시스템 ('information system'), 온라인 ('online'), 스마트 ('smart')), reported for both lexicons.

3.4 Embedding alignment

All 326,592 normalization keys were embedded with a multilingual open-weight model (BAAI/bge-m3, Hugging Face revision 5617a9f6, dense output, L2-normalised, batch 128, sentence-transformers 5.1.2 / torch 2.8.0 on Apple M4 Max (MPS); the second encoder of §4.8 is intfloat/multilingual-e5-large, revision 3d7cfbda, with the "query: " prefix) run locally, and all pairs with cosine ≥ .85 were collected (586,498 pairs). Clusters were formed at three thresholds (.85/.90/.95) by two methods: transitive closure (single linkage) and Louvain community detection on the similarity graph. We report both because they differ qualitatively (§4.3). Recovery is expressed against surface forms so that it is directly comparable with the rule stage: combined recovery = (surface forms − final clusters) / surface forms.

3.5 Replication corpus

To test whether the rule/model boundary is a property of NCS or of competency standards as a text type, we re-ran the fragmentation (RQ3) and taxonomy (RQ4) analyses, and a template analogue of RQ1, on the European ESCO classification, version 1.2.1, English release [20] (official CSV distribution; 13,960 released skill concepts, 3,039 occupations, 619 ISCO groups; 67,600 essential and 58,451 optional occupation–skill relations). We verified the release version against the published v1.2.0→v1.2.1 change log: 1,105 of 1,105 English alternative labels added in v1.2.1 are present, and 38 of 1,182 removed labels remain (3.2%). ESCO differs from NCS in one way that matters for this paper: it records for every skill concept a preferred label and its alternative labels (86,690 English alternative labels), so the fragmentation analysis has a ground truth. Two surface forms denote the same concept if and only if they are labels of the same skill URI. Precision of rule and embedding merges can therefore be measured exhaustively rather than sampled, and the NCS human-validation estimates of §4.6 can be checked against an exact reference. ESCO has no "can-do" template; as the closest analogue we test the documented labelling convention that skill/competence labels begin with a verb and knowledge labels with a noun, tagged with a general-domain English part-of-speech model (spaCy en_core_web_sm). Rule normalization was re-specified for English (R0: case-folding and punctuation removal; R1: R0 plus a crude inflectional suffix strip). Everything else — encoder, similarity thresholds, pair collection, single-linkage and Louvain clustering, the Jaccard occupation graph, and the permutation null — is the NCS code unchanged.

4.1 Performance criteria are almost perfectly templated (RQ1)

Of 196,658 performance criteria, 195,799 (99.563%; a census quantity, reported without an interval) end in the canonical form; the median occupation is at 100%. The 859 deviations are typed as declarative endings (504), template variants occurring mid-sentence (245), other (93), and nominal endings (17). Deviations are concentrated: half of them lie in 26 occupations (2.3% of the corpus), and one occupation (환경미화 ('environmental sanitation'); we name it because the finding is about a public document, not a person) has 0% compliance, indicating an occupation-level drafting convention rather than scattered noise.

Implication. The compliance rate itself is close to a tautology — the NCS drafting guideline prescribes the "~할 수 있다" ending, so 99.56% measures how faithfully authors followed an explicit rule, and we report it as a descriptive fact. The finding is the locality of what escaped: the 859 deviations are not scattered noise but concentrate in 26 occupations, and one occupation ignores the template entirely. For a pipeline this means that syntactic parsing of performance criteria can be handled by deterministic rules everywhere except at an enumerable list of locations, which can be routed to a model or to review before any model is trained. It also means the list is a maintenance artifact: it names where the standard departs from its own guideline.

4.2 Redundancy is an industry property, not a size artifact (RQ2)

Across the corpus only 16.07% of KSA items are distinct strings. Because uniqueness rate is mechanically dependent on set size, we regressed it on log(KSA items) and log(competency units) (R² = .369) and tested the between-major effect on the residuals. The effect does not shrink under control — it grows (ε² = .158 → .191; Kruskal H = 229.9, p < .0001), replicating in both halves. Industries whose residual uniqueness is lowest are Finance & Insurance (금융·보험), Machinery (기계), Food Processing (식품가공); highest are Sales (영업판매), Business Management (사업관리), Textiles & Apparel (섬유·의복).

Implication. Deduplication yields a 5–10× reduction whose magnitude is predictable from the industry, which is what a build-cost estimate for candidate judgment or human review actually needs.

4.3 Fragmentation defeats rules and only partly yields to embeddings (RQ3)

355,896 distinct surface forms reduce to 326,592 normalization keys — a fragmentation index of 1.09 and a rule-stage recovery of just 8.2% of surface forms, far below the pre-registered 50–70%. The other half of H3 is strongly supported: fragmentation grows with how widely a concept is used (ρ = .539 between a key's occupation count and its number of surface forms, p < .0001, n = 326,592). The most fragmented keys are ordinary shared competencies — 통계분석 ('statistical analysis') (25 surface forms across 101 occupations), 품질관리 ('quality control') (24/98), 도면해독 ('drawing interpretation') (23/104) — and 6,310 keys (1.9%) are assigned to more than one KSA type, i.e. the same concept is filed as knowledge in one occupation and as skill in another.

The reason rules fail is structural: normalization keys absorb whitespace, punctuation, and role suffixes, so "통계 분석 지식 ('knowledge of statistical analysis')" and "통계분석기법 ('statistical-analysis techniques')" merge, but variants with different stems — 안전사항 준수 ('compliance with safety matters'), 안전수칙 준수 ('compliance with safety rules'), 안전규정 준수 ('compliance with safety regulations') — do not. Embedding alignment addresses exactly this residue.

The 8.2% is not an artifact of the particular suffix list: five list variants move rule recovery only from 3.5% to 9.0%, saturating near 9% while type-mismatch rises, so rules in this corpus saturate whatever the list (Supplementary S1).

Merge participation rises with frequency exactly as the ρ = .539 relation predicts: 83.6% of keys used by ≥ 10 occupations are merged, versus 64.5% (3–9), 48.8% (2), and 28.7% (1).

A measured methodological caution. Our first clustering used transitive closure. At cos ≥ .90 this produced a single component of 17,118 keys spanning 1,106 of 1,109 occupations, containing items as unrelated as "data 처리 및 분석 ('data processing and analysis')" and "HW 요구사항을 명확히 정의하고자 하는 자세 ('attitude of defining HW requirements clearly')"; at cos ≥ .85 a single component absorbed 29.9% of all keys. Louvain community detection on the same similarity graph reduced the largest cluster to 1,084 while leaving aggregate recovery essentially unchanged (28.2% → 28.1%). The chain therefore corrupts which keys are merged without changing how many — an error that aggregate recovery statistics alone cannot detect. We report both and use the modularity-based estimate.

Merging is somewhat more common among attitude items (38.2%) than skills (28.6%) or knowledge (27.9%), and the largest clusters are attitude families — "기준·법규 준수 ('compliance with standards and regulations')" (1,084 keys, 636 occupations), "개인보호구 착용·안전 준수 ('wearing protective equipment and safety compliance')" (1,016), "객관적 분석 ('objective analysis')" (992) — whose formulaic phrasing ("~하려는 자세/의지 ('attitude / will to …')") plausibly lets syntactic similarity contribute. Roughly 70% of merged keys are nonetheless knowledge or skill items. Manual precision of the merges is assessed in §4.6.

4.4 Shared vocabulary partially recovers the official taxonomy (RQ4)

An occupation graph built from the 25,078 normalization keys that occur in two or more occupations (1,107 nodes — two occupations share no key with any other — and 190,355 weighted edges) yields 11 Louvain communities. Agreement with the 24 official major categories is NMI = .329 (ARI = .108), against a permutation null of .041 ± .004 (p < .001); agreement with the 81 middle categories is NMI = .356. Removing generic keys that appear in ≥ 10% of occupations leaves the result intact (NMI = .341), so the agreement is not an artifact of cross-industry boilerplate such as safety compliance.

The pre-registered expectation (NMI between .3 and .6) is met (Table 1), and we report it as registered. The result is stable across similarity measure and Louvain resolution (NMI .33–.56, AMI .30–.51; Supplementary S1). It was, in hindsight, too permissive to be a sharp test, so we add — and label as post-hoc sharpening, formed after the results were seen — the two one-sided rejections that give H4 its falsifiable content. Against the hypothesis that shared vocabulary carries no taxonomic information, the permutation null of .041 ± .004 is exceeded by every cell of the sensitivity grid below (minimum non-degenerate NMI .33, AMI .30). Against the hypothesis that shared vocabulary is the taxonomy — that the official categories could be reconstructed from KSA overlap — the maximum agreement reached anywhere in the grid is NMI .56 / AMI .51 / ARI .29, with 40% of occupations more similar to another major category's occupations than to their own (below). "Partial" therefore means: far from chance, far from complete, and the informative result is not the value but which categories fail to cohere.

The Jaccard @ 1.0 cell reproduces the main analysis to within Louvain's ordering variance (NMI .331 vs. .329; the main run's 11 communities and null of .041 are otherwise identical).

4.5 Level, revision recency, and digital vocabulary (RQ5)

The three remaining pre-registered hypotheses (H5, H6, S4) and the two descriptive ones (S1, S2) concern how competency level, revision recency, digital vocabulary, and the attitude lexicon are distributed across industries. They are reported in full in Appendix A so that the pre-registration record stays complete, and summarised here because they matter to a pipeline only as stratification variables. Level differs sharply by industry (ε² = .242; L5+ share from 71.6% in Finance & Insurance to 7.3% in Security & Cleaning); revision recency is uneven but less than pre-registered (ε² = .175 against .30); digital vocabulary varies by an order of magnitude across industries but is unrelated to recency within them (ρ = .077 lexicon A, .098 for a lexicon with the ambiguous terms 프로그램 and 전산 removed), so the amount of digital language is a property of the industry rather than of when the standard was last revised; the pre-registered expectation that compliance dominates the attitude lexicon is not met, though neither does volition dominate once a coding artifact is removed (Appendix A); and the pre-registered coincidence of low level, stale revision, and low digital vocabulary is not supported (W = .449, p = .123). We report these as corpus properties a pipeline should expect — inputs to sampling, stratification, and coverage — and stop there: whether they reflect or shape labour-market conditions cannot be decided from the standard alone; answering it requires external wage, qualification, and employment data that this study does not use.

4.6 Manual precision of embedding merges

Recovery counts what the embedding stage proposes; whether the proposals are correct requires human judgment. We sampled 200 merged pairs in two arms: 120 directly linked pairs stratified by cosine band (.85–.90, .90–.95, .95–1.0; 40 each), which measure the similarity signal itself and ground the threshold choice, and 80 transitively linked pairs — co-members of a cos ≥ .90 Louvain cluster with no direct edge — which measure what cluster membership implies. Eight raters external to the authorship (two PhDs in educational technology, three university staff, three students of unrelated majors) each judged a common anchor set of 50 pairs plus a 30-pair block balanced across strata (80 pairs each; 640 judgments); the first author judged all 200 for a bias check only. Raters saw, for each side, up to four surface forms of the key, its KSA type, its occupation count, and the names of up to three major categories in which it occurs; they did not see similarity, cluster size, or arm. The instruction defined identity relative to the construction task — "if merging the two would lose information, they are not the same concept" — so the construct is mergeability into one node, not synonymy in general. Judgments used five categories — same concept, hierarchical, related-but-distinct, unrelated, unsure — from which we derive strict precision (same) and lenient precision (same or hierarchical, since a hierarchical pair belongs in the ontology as a relation rather than a merge). Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over items, because judgments of the same pair are not independent.

(ii) The pre-registered threshold is the precision knee in both corpora: direct-edge precision rises from 57.0% in the .85–.90 band to 88.6% in .90–.95 on ESCO, mirroring the 43.8% → 71.1% step estimated by raters on NCS. What does not transfer. Two findings are NCS-specific. The 99.56% syntactic template has no counterpart: ESCO skill labels begin with a verb 79.9% of the time under a general-domain tagger, so the near-total regularity of NCS performance criteria is a property of how NCS was authored, not of competency text as such. And the absolute recovery figures are corpus-level: ESCO is far more fragmented by design (7.2 labels per concept, registered as such), so Louvain at .90 absorbs 62.7% of its surfaces at 95% precision, against 28.1% at 48% on NCS. The ESCO result also bounds the encoder from the other side: with an exact reference, cos ≥ .90 retrieves only 34.8% of true synonym pairs, so even where precision is high, two-thirds of the registered synonymy is invisible to embedding similarity at the safe threshold. Both corpora therefore agree that the residual fragmentation is the work of the concept layer, not a calibration problem.

The replication also calibrates the NCS rater estimates. ESCO's exact precision exceeds the NCS strict estimate in every band by 13–25 points, and its lenient counterpart (§4.6: 59.4 / 82.8 / 87.5%) lies closer to the ESCO values (57.0 / 88.6 / 98.7%). The likeliest reading is that ESCO's alternative labels are curated paraphrases of one concept, whereas NCS surface forms are independently authored sentences that raters legitimately place in "related" or "hierarchical" rather than "same"; the strict NCS figures are conservative for that corpus, not evidence of rater harshness.

4.8 Encoder sensitivity: the knee is a property of the task

All embedding results above use one encoder. Re-running the alignment with a second, unrelated encoder (multilingual-e5-large) and comparing at matched pair counts — because cosine scales are not comparable across encoders — reproduces every aggregate to within a point: the precision knee, the transitive collapse, the chaining failure, the ESCO precision bands, and the NCS recovery. The agreement is at the level of aggregates, not pairs: the two encoders share about half of the NCS pairs they select, and pairs chosen by both are markedly more precise (83% against 76% for one encoder), a cheap precision lever. 4.9 Does the measurement change a decision? Three downstream tasks in two corpora

The numbers above are only useful if a pipeline built with them differs from one built without them. We therefore expressed each corpus under several concept-layer policies — normalization keys only (the rule stage); embedding merges accepted at cos ≥ .90 with community-size caps of 2, 5, 20, or none; and the two-encoder-agreement variant of §4.8 — and ran a task that a knowledge resource built from a competency standard is actually asked to do. The primary evidence is on ESCO, where the reference is external and exact: skill-label search, in which one registered alternative label per skill is removed from the index and used as the query, and the system must return the occupations for which that skill is essential. On NCS no external reference exists, so the two tasks there — occupation similarity search (the five nearest occupations by overlap of concept sets; precision at 5 for sharing the minor and the middle category) and taxonomy recovery (the community procedure of §4.4) — score agreement with the official classification, the same classification §4.4 showed to be only partially reflected in the vocabulary. We therefore read the NCS results as coherence with the taxonomy a practitioner must navigate, not as quality, and place them second.

Where the gain comes from, and how large it is. Two facts about the key inventory decide what the embedding stage can do downstream. First, 92.3% of the 326,592 normalization keys occur in a single occupation, and a key that occurs in one occupation contributes to the denominator of every Jaccard comparison involving that occupation but never to a numerator. Second, single-occupation keys are merged in proportion to their share of the inventory, not more: at cap ≤ 5, 65,557 keys are merged and 89.8% of them (58,873) are single-occupation keys, slightly below their 92.3% share — consistent with the merge participation of 28.7% for single-occupation keys against 83.6% for widely used ones in §4.3. There is no enrichment to read the effect off; only an ablation can show whether these keys carry it. We therefore re-ran the cap ≤ 5 and ≤ 20 policies with single-occupation keys excluded from merging. The entire downstream gain disappears: under Jaccard, P@5 falls from 31.8% to 28.8% (rules 28.6%) and NMI from .360 to .293 (rules .301); under TF-IDF, P@5 falls from 33.8% to 31.4% (rules 31.2%), and the paired difference between merging and not merging single-occupation keys is +2.42 points [+1.80, +3.05] over the same 1,109 occupations. What embedding alignment does for a downstream consumer of this corpus is therefore specific: it pulls expressions that appear once into concepts that are shared, and it does almost nothing else — keys that are merged at no more than their base rate carry the whole of the downstream effect, which is why the ablation, not the merge counts, is the informative test. The size of that effect, stated plainly, is small on the similarity task and nil on taxonomy recovery. Under TF-IDF the paired gain of cap ≤ 5 over rules alone is +2.63 P@5 points [+1.97, +3.32] and of cap ≤ 20 +3.25 [+2.49, +4.00], both clearly non-zero; NMI moves from .393 to .412, inside the seed-to-seed range of either policy. On NCS, then, the concept layer is worth two to three points of retrieval precision once generic concepts are downweighted, and the evidence that the layer changes a decision rests on ESCO, where the reference is external — which is why Table 7 is placed first.

The single-occupation keys that merge go to three places, and the three are not alike. At cap ≤ 5, 29,712 of them merge with another key of the same occupation, 21,083 with a single-occupation key of a different occupation — creating a shared concept that did not exist — and 8,078 join a concept some other occupation already holds. Adding each path alone to the no-singleton policy shows that the cross-occupation path carries the gain (+2.04 P@5 points [+1.48, +2.60] under TF-IDF, 84% of the total), joining an existing concept adds a little (+0.49 [+0.02, +0.96]), and merging within an occupation adds nothing (+0.07 [−0.04, +0.20]). Classifying the first-round judged pairs the same way gives the precision of each operation on direct edges — 72.5% [60.2, 82.7] for joining an existing concept (37 items), 61.1% [47.2, 76.1] for a cross-occupation single-key pair (46), and 43.9% [29.5, 60.3] for a pair within one occupation (28) — so the operation that carries the downstream gain is also the one that creates an edge where none existed and does so at the lower precision, while within-occupation merging is both useless downstream and the least precise, plausibly because an author who wrote two similar items for one occupation had already distinguished them. The item counts per path are small and the intervals wide; the ordering, not the point values, is what we rely on.

What the cap is for. The community-size cap therefore governs one thing and not another. It governs the precision of merges written into the concept layer: 66% at size ≤ 5 against 55% for all merges (§4.6). That is a claim about the artifact, not about a task — under weighting none of the three tasks we measured separates the caps — so we do not offer the cap as a performance lever but as a quality standard for what a builder ships. We therefore release the two stages as two files. The alias dictionary contains rule-stage groups only, one per normalization key carrying at least two surface forms, and the corresponding rules are 97.8–98.9% precise on ESCO (§4.7). The embedding stage is released separately as a labelled candidate file built at cap ≤ 5 (27,721 groups, 65,557 keys), each row carrying its merge path, direct-edge share, the precision the §4.6 strata imply, and the raters' verdicts where a member pair was judged, so that the 66% of Table 4 is attached to the artifact rather than to a sentence. It does not govern downstream similarity once generic concepts are downweighted, and the task-optimal cap (≤ 20 in both NCS tasks) is not the precision-optimal one (≤ 5). Merge precision is thus not a proxy for downstream utility, which is the third methodological lesson of this paper alongside the chaining artifact of §4.3 and the resolution caution of §5. The recommendation that follows is in four parts: downweight generic concepts in any similarity computed over the layer; exclude within-occupation merges from the default automatic policy, since in the tasks we measured that path has no downstream value and the lowest precision; accept a merge that joins single-occupation keys across occupations only on a direct edge, at cap ≤ 5 where the layer is published or written unreviewed and ≤ 20 where a reviewer or a weighted consumer follows, because that path carries the gain and creates the edges that are wrong when it errs; and expand at query time by direct edges rather than by community membership. The strongest retrieval result in this paper uses no concept layer at all (Table 7), and it is worth saying plainly what a persisted layer is then for. It is not justified by retrieval performance but by what a query-time neighbour lookup cannot give: a deduplication estimate before a build is costed (§4.2), taxonomy recovery and the categories that fail to cohere (§4.4), the maintainer-facing audit list (§4.1), a stable target for mapping one corpus onto another, and the released dictionary itself.

5. Discussion

The four measurements compose into a single design rule for this corpus type: structure to rules, concepts to models. Syntax is 99.56% regular and its residue is confined to 26 occupations, so rules parse it and a model is needed only at named locations. Concepts are the opposite: rules repair 8.2% of fragmentation because the variation lives in stems, not in affixes, and even a strong multilingual encoder proposes 28.1%, of which human raters confirm 19.2–23.6%; the remainder is concept families, not concepts. The residual fragmentation is not noise to be tuned away; it is the actual work of building a concept layer, and its size — and the precision of the tools that attack it — is now known before that work begins. §4.9 shows what knowing it buys — and what it does not. The cap protects the precision of what is written into the layer; downstream similarity over that layer is governed by downweighting generic concepts, without which unrestricted merging flattens every occupation onto the same attitude families; and a query-time index built on communities rather than direct edges gives away six points of precision for nothing. What the embedding stage adds downstream is specific and small: it links expressions that occur in one occupation to concepts held by others, and within-occupation variants should not be merged automatically without independent evidence, since that path adds nothing downstream and is the least precise of the three (§4.9).

A second lesson concerns the resolution at which such corpora are described. Every exploratory pattern in this study looked dramatic at the level of 24 major categories and shrank when tested across 1,109 occupations: revision recency (ε² = .175 against a pre-registered .30), the digital-recency association (ρ = .077 against .2), and the coincidence of disadvantages (W = .449, n.s.). Aggregate views of national standards — the form in which they are usually discussed — systematically overstate between-industry contrasts because within-industry variance is invisible in them. This is a caution for policy analyses built on category-level summaries as much as for pipeline design.

Third, the clustering artifact reported in §4.3 has a general moral for pipelines that align terminology by embedding similarity. Single-linkage merging is the default in many practical systems because it is cheap and order-independent; here it silently produced a component containing a third of the corpus while the headline recovery statistic barely moved. Aggregate recovery is therefore not a sufficient quality signal for an alignment stage, and cluster-size distribution should be reported alongside it.

The ESCO replication (§4.7) settles which of these lessons are about NCS and which are about competency standards. The division of labour and the alignment cautions transfer with the same shape in a second language and a second framework, and with an exact rather than sampled reference: rules top out near 9%, precision breaks at .90, transitive merges are right only 30–40% of the time, and single-linkage at .85 swallows a third of the corpus. The template finding does not transfer, and the absolute recovery figures do not either — ESCO's registered synonymy is five times denser and its merges are twice as precise. A pipeline designer can therefore carry the rules of this paper to another framework, but must re-measure the levels; the released code makes that measurement a matter of hours.

Finally, the results bound what a downstream construction pipeline must do. Candidate concepts number in the hundreds of thousands even after normalization; deduplication is worth 5–10× and its size is industry-predictable; alignment at a safe threshold is automatable, while the larger recovery band produces families whose merger is a judgment rather than a computation. Human judgment of that question is itself only moderately reliable — α = .41 for the five-way distinction and .50 for the merge decision among four domain experts, with pairwise values ranging from .34 to .57 — so the adjudication stage must be designed for disagreement: majority voting over several judges and an explicit abstention category rather than single verdicts. That judgment — not the extraction — is where human or model adjudication belongs.

What the measurements say about the input itself. Read strictly from the construction side, three of the results also describe where the corpus's properties come from, and a pipeline designer should know this because it predicts how the numbers will move across releases and across sectors. First, redundancy and the attitude vocabulary are authored, not accidental: KSA duplication is an industry-level property that survives size control (§4.2), and compliance sentences are a small set replicated across occupations while volition sentences are written afresh (§4.5). NCS is drafted sector by sector, and the corpus carries the fingerprints of separate authoring practices; a pipeline should therefore expect its deduplication factor and its alignment load to vary by sector, not by corpus size. Second, the large embedding communities are not chains of unrelated concepts, as they are on ESCO, but families of sentences that express one generic disposition in occupation-specific wording (§4.6, §4.7): the standard produces apparent specificity at the attitude layer. For construction this means that attitude items should be aligned to a small shared inventory rather than treated as occupation-specific concepts. Third, the contrast with ESCO is a contrast of design, not only of language: ESCO is a curated, label-centred concept graph, NCS an authored, sentence-centred hierarchy, and that difference — not the encoder and not the threshold — explains why rule normalization, transitive precision, and the size gradient behave as they do in each. Finally, the by-products of the analysis (the alias dictionary, the 26 template-deviant occupations, the typographical variants the alignment surfaced, the revision-year dispersion) constitute a quality-audit list for whoever maintains the standard; we release it as an artifact and draw no evaluative conclusion from it here.

6. Threats to Validity

Construct. "Same concept" was judged as mergeability into one node under the stated instruction, with the category context described in §4.6; a different purpose (job matching, curriculum mapping) could draw the line elsewhere, and the amount of hierarchy shown to judges was fixed rather than varied. The code-year field records when a unit was last developed or revised, not the magnitude of the revision; "freshness" therefore means recency of attention, not extent of change. The digital lexicon is keyword-based; a lexicon with the ambiguous terms (프로그램, 전산) removed gives the same conclusions (Appendix A). The attitude codebook is rule-coded, and we report a failure of it: its categories overlap by construction (43.7% of sentences match more than one), and although the ranking is invariant to the priority order that resolves the overlap, it is not invariant to the keyword lists — 46.6% of the volition category's mass rests on three generic nominalizers, and removing them levels the top two categories (Appendix A). We therefore treat the S2 distribution as a description of the pre-registered instrument rather than of the corpus, and rest no claim on which category leads. Because no conclusion in this paper depends on that instrument, we did not run a double-coded validity check of the keyword lists: an inter-coder κ would add evidence about a tool whose output we deliberately do not use. Internal. Normalization-key rules are a design choice; the code is released and suffix-list sensitivity is reported (§4.3: 3.5–9.0% across five lists, saturating near 9%); rule-merge precision is measured only on ESCO (97.8–98.9%), not on NCS. Taxonomy-recovery statistics depend on Louvain's resolution; §4.4 reports the grid, and the chance-corrected range (AMI .30–.51) rather than the single pre-registered cell is the defensible summary. Embedding results are reported for one encoder, but §4.8 shows that a second encoder of a different lineage reproduces every aggregate at matched pair counts; the encoder's tendency to score formulaic attitude sentences as similar is quantified in §4.3, tested manually in §4.6, and unchanged under the second encoder (38.7% vs. 38.2%). Matching by pair count equalizes recall, not the pairs themselves — the two encoders share only half the NCS pairs they select — so per-pair conclusions remain encoder-specific. Manual validation rests on fourteen non-author raters over three rounds with an expert reliability ceiling of α = .41 (five-way) / .50 (merge decision) over four experts — a figure we could only obtain by adding the third round, and one that is lower than the .57 / .68 the first expert pair alone produced; the second round, which supplies the precision for communities above 20 keys (60 items, three judgments each), used four raters without domain experience whose mutual agreement is low (α = .17 / .26), so that stratum's estimate is a pooled rate with a ±9-point interval rather than a consensus label; raters did not use the free-text memo field, so disagreements are quantified but not explained. One of the 50 anchor items also appears as a worked example in the rater instructions, which could prime it; dropping it moves the expert α from .569 to .554 (five-way) and from .683 to .677 (merge decision), and the eight-rater α by at most .010, so the effect is negligible and we keep the item for comparability across rounds. Items were shuffled once and then shown in the same fixed order to every rater, so any fatigue or position effect is common to all raters and cannot be separated from item difficulty; median time per judgment fell from 12.0 s in the first quarter of a session to 8.0 s in the last. Inferential. Six of nine hypotheses were formed after seeing major-category aggregates (PEEK) and are labelled as such; confirmatory tests use a resolution exploration did not observe, split-half replication, and FDR, but this is weaker than a fully blind pre-registration. External. The primary corpus is one framework, one language, one release. The ESCO replication (§4.7) extends the fragmentation and taxonomy findings to a second language and framework, but it is a replication of RQ3 and RQ4 only: ESCO has no performance-criteria layer, so RQ1 is tested by a weaker analogue (label-initial part of speech under a general-domain tagger, whose errors on imperative-form labels are unmeasured), and RQ2 and RQ5 have no counterpart. ESCO's ground truth is also a different construct from the NCS raters' judgment — registered alternative labels of one URI rather than a five-way human verdict — which is the likeliest reason its precision runs 13–25 points above the strict NCS estimates. O*NET remains untested.

7. Conclusion

Measuring a national competency standard before building on it changes what one builds. In Korea's NCS, the syntactic residue that rules cannot parse is small and enumerable (859 criteria, half in 26 occupations), while concept identity is not a rule-tractable problem: rules repair 8.2% of terminological fragmentation and embedding alignment proposes 28.1%, of which an estimated 19.2–23.6% survives human validation under the study's conservative mergeability definition and rater panel; the remainder are concept families whose merger requires adjudication, and the .90 threshold is confirmed as the precision knee. Occupational structure is partially latent in shared vocabulary alone (NMI = .329 vs. .041 null), and industry differences in level, revision recency, and digital language are real but considerably smaller at occupation resolution than category-level summaries suggest. On ESCO, with an exact reference, the rule ceiling, the .90 knee, the ~30% transitive precision, the chaining failure, and partial taxonomy recovery reproduce; the syntactic template does not. Concept identity is contested in a specific way rather than generally: across four educational-technology PhDs judging the same 50 pairs, the share called the same concept spans 28.0–48.0% while the share called related at all spans only 62.0–66.0%, so what separates expert from expert is a single boundary and not a reading of the corpus — a property of the instrument that anyone designing human adjudication for an alignment pipeline inherits. The measured policies change outcomes: generic-concept downweighting governs downstream similarity; query-time expansion should use direct edges rather than communities; the downstream value of embedding alignment lies in linking single-occupation expressions across occupations, so within-occupation merges belong outside the default automatic policy; and the merge cap governs not those tasks but the precision of what is written into a published concept layer. We release the metrics, the alias dictionary, the embedding-stage candidate file, the ESCO replication, the downstream tasks, and the code.

Appendix A. Level, revision recency, and digital vocabulary (RQ5) — full report

Pre-registered hypotheses H5, H6, S4 and descriptive S1, S2; summarised in §4.5. Reported in full for completeness of the pre-registration record.

Competency levels differ sharply by industry (ε² = .242): the share of units at L5 or above ranges from 71.6% (Finance & Insurance), 67.2% (Education, Natural & Social Sciences), 65.4% (Information & Communication Technology) down to 10.8% (Food Processing), 8.6% (Food Service (음식서비스)), 7.3% (Security & Cleaning).

Revision recency is uneven but less so than pre-registered: mean code year ranges from 2021.7 (Management, Accounting & Office Work (경영·회계·사무)) to 2015.3 (Finance & Insurance), Gini = .314, with a clear but moderate between-industry effect (ε² = .175, below the pre-registered .30). Digital vocabulary varies by an order of magnitude across industries (10.9% Information & Communication Technology to 0.24% Security & Cleaning; ε² = .208 for the distribution), but the pre-registered within-industry association between recency and digital vocabulary is negligible (ρ = .077, p = .010) and did not replicate across halves; lexicon B gives the same conclusion (ρ = .078, p = .009). The amount of digital language a standard contains is thus a property of the industry, not of when the standard was last revised; the three industries that combine high digital vocabulary with old code years are Information & Communication Technology, Finance & Insurance, and Culture, Arts, Design & Broadcasting.

The attitude lexicon did not behave as pre-registered. By item mass the leading category is volition/effort (38.5%), with compliance/safety second (20.4%), then cognition (13.6%), quality (9.9%), interpersonal (6.7%), and ethics (6.0%); between-industry differences are significant but small (V = .125). That ranking is registered as computed, but it does not survive inspection of what drives it. Decomposing the volition category by the keyword that placed each sentence there, 46.6% of its mass comes from sentences matched only on 자세 ('stance'), 마음 ('mind') or 태도 ('attitude') — the conventional nominalizers with which attitude statements are written in this corpus, not expressions of volition. Setting those three aside leaves volition at 20.6% against compliance at 20.4%: the two are level, and the honest reading is that the pre-registered expectation fails because no category dominates, not because volition displaces compliance. The exploratory impression that compliance dominates came from a different weighting — by occupational breadth, where 안전사항 준수 (172 occupations) and 안전수칙 준수 (134) lead. The two weightings answer different questions, and the difference can be seen without any categorisation at all. Among the 82,700 distinct attitude sentences (95,237 occupation–sentence items), sentences containing 준수 ('compliance') number 10,502 and carry 13,481 items — 1.28 items per sentence, with the five commonest accounting for 4.0% of them. Sentences containing 노력 ('effort') number 12,079 and carry 13,131 items — 1.09 per sentence, top five 0.9%. 안전 ('safety') sits at 1.34 per sentence and 6.9%. Compliance vocabulary is thus carried by fewer, more widely replicated sentences and volition vocabulary by more, rarely repeated ones, which is what the two weightings pick up; the effect is real but modest, and much smaller than the 38.5%-versus-20.4% category split suggested. The codebook assigns each sentence to the first matching category, so its priority order is a design choice that matters: 43.7% of the 95,237 attitude sentences match more than one category. The reported ranking does not depend on that choice, and the pre-registered order is in fact the one least favourable to it — compliance is first in the list and volition last, yet volition leads 38.5% to 20.4%. Reversing the order gives 79.4% to 6.4%, counting only sentences that match exactly one category gives 75.0% to 12.5% (n = 48,949), and volition is the leading category in all 200 random orderings of the six. The priority order is therefore not what produces the ranking; the keyword list is. Finally, the pre-registered coincidence of low level, stale revision, and low digital vocabulary was not supported (Kendall's W = .449, p = .123 — the only hypothesis not surviving FDR), although the same five industries occupy the bottom of the composite index (Security & Cleaning, Food Processing, Textiles & Apparel, Agriculture, Forestry & Fisheries (농림어업), Driving & Transport (운전·운송)).

Lexicon robustness. Lexicon B is a superset of lexicon A, so their agreement is not a test. A third lexicon, C, removes from A the two ambiguous terms (프로그램, which also denotes an educational programme, and 전산): the between-industry effect is if anything larger (ε² = .256 vs. .208), the within-industry association with recency remains negligible (ρ = .098, p = .001), and per-occupation shares under A and C correlate at ρ = .83. The conclusion does not depend on the ambiguous terms.

S2. Human validation in detail: rater groups, the author's judgments, and context bias (§4.6)

The second round in detail. Because that last stratum carries the most absorbed keys (22,874) on the fewest items, we ran a second, targeted round: 60 new transitive pairs from communities of 21–100 and above 100 keys (30 each), judged by three of four new non-author raters each (180 judgments). Precision there is 41.7% strict [32.2, 50.6] and 80.6% lenient — 41.1% and 42.2% strict in the two strata — so in NCS transitive precision does not fall with community size; it sits at 30–42% throughout. The large communities are dominated by attitude sentences (20 of the 60 pairs are attitude–attitude, 60% strict) and paraphrase families ("검사 방법 절차 ('inspection method procedure')" / "테스트 절차 ('test procedure')", "타 부서와 협동하려는 태도 ('attitude of cooperating with other departments')" / "관련 부서간의 협력적 태도 ('cooperative attitude among related departments')"), not by chained unrelated concepts.

Concept identity in this corpus is a contested judgment even for domain experts, and more so than a two-rater estimate suggested. The first round measured the ceiling with one pair of educational-technology PhDs (α = .569 five-way, .683 merge). A third round put the same 50 anchor items to two further PhDs from outside the university under identical conditions, giving four experts and six pairs: the ceiling falls to α = .408 [.285, .520] five-way and .503 [.352, .635] for the merge decision, and the original pair turns out to be the most concordant of the six (pairwise five-way α ranges .335–.569, median .396). A two-rater ceiling is therefore optimistic by construction — it estimates one draw from the space of expert pairs, and we drew a good one. Nor does reliability order neatly by expertise: the four experts (.408) are indistinguishable from the three staff (.422, overlapping intervals) on the same items, and both exceed the students (.289) and the second-round raters without domain exposure (.174). What experience separates is having some domain exposure from having none, not experts from administrators. The unsure rate is low (2.3%), so raters committed rather than abstained. The second-round raters, none with domain experience, agree with each other least (α = .17 / .26); on the ten anchor pairs they share with the first round their strict rate is 45% against 50% for the first-round raters on the same items, and their lenient rate 85% against 75% — they reach for "hierarchical" more often (31–50% of verdicts vs. 18%) but not for "same", so the strict second-round estimate is not inflated by rater leniency. The two expert pairs disagree in a specific and systematic way rather than randomly. On the anchor items the first pair calls 48.0% of pairs the same concept and the second 29.0%, yet both reach almost the same figure once hierarchical pairs are added (63.0% and 65.0%): they place the boundary between "same" and "hierarchical" in different places while agreeing on what is unrelated. The effect is individual rather than a property of the two pairs: taken one by one the four experts span 28.0–48.0% on "same" yet only 62.0–66.0% once hierarchical pairs are counted, so what separates them is a single boundary and not their reading of the corpus. This is the kind of systematic rater effect that two raters cannot expose, and it has a consequence for the precision estimates. Experts are stricter than the other groups: on the same 50 anchors the four PhDs call 38.5% of pairs the same concept, against 52.7% for staff and 44.7% for students. Restricted to the 20 anchor items at cos ≥ .90, expert strict precision is 62.5% against the 72.3% pooled over all eight first-round raters and 120 direct-edge items. The expert subsample is small, but it points one way: if expert judgment is taken as the standard, verified recovery falls from 19.2% to 18.2%, and to 16.9% if transitive precision is scaled down in the same proportion. We retain the pooled estimates as the headline because they rest on the larger sample and the pre-registered design, and record the expert-only figures as the lower end of the range.

How much weight does the thinnest evidence carry? The communities above 20 keys hold 22,874 of the 70,869 absorbed keys, and their transitive precision comes from four raters whose mutual agreement is low (§4.6, α = .17). Because fewer than one pair in ten inside those communities is a direct edge, the figure that this stratum can move is bounded: fixing its transitive precision at 0% gives a verified recovery of 16.6%, at the lower and upper 95% limits of the second-round estimate 18.4% and 20.1%, and at 100% 22.8%. The headline is therefore 19.2% under the measured value and lies between 16.6% and 22.8% under any value at all; a pooled proportion is unbiased under low inter-rater agreement, which widens its interval but does not shift it. The stratum carries a visible, not a decisive, share of the conclusion.

The first author's judgments do not indicate leniency toward the method: strict precision 55.5% [48.5, 62.0] against the external 49.7% [42.8, 56.4], merge-decision agreement with the external majority 74.3% (κ = .475), and symmetric disagreements (18 pairs the externals called the same and the author did not; 21 the reverse). Author judgments are excluded from all estimates above. Contested pairs are genuinely hard near-misses — whole-genome versus whole-exome sequencing data analysis (cos .884), 3D modelling versus 3D analysis software (cos .890) — while one unanimous "same" pair, ISO/IEC 27009 (인증섹터 ('certification sector')) versus (인증센터 ('certification centre')), is a typographical error in the standard that the alignment surfaced and the raters confirmed, an instance of the quality-audit use in §1.

Did the displayed context steer the judgments? Because raters saw the major categories of each key, a reviewer may ask whether pairs from different industries were judged "different" on that cue rather than on meaning. The data do not show that bias: pairs whose keys share no major category were judged "same" at least as often as pairs that share one, in every stratum (direct edges .85–.90: 50.0% vs. 36.2%; .90–.95: 72.4% vs. 68.3%; ≥ .95: 70.2% vs. 75.3%; transitive, first round: 34.8% vs. 22.8%; second round: 41.7% vs. 41.7%), and the per-rater direction is inconsistent (four of eight raters were stricter on cross-category pairs, four more lenient). The comparison is confounded — keys used by many occupations list more categories and are more often generic — so it shows the absence of the feared direction rather than the absence of any context effect. The displayed cue that does track judgments is KSA type: direct pairs whose keys carry the same type are judged the same concept 65.4% of the time against 31.0% when the types differ (transitive: 35.5% vs. 15.7%). Under the instruction above a knowledge item and a skill item about the same object are legitimately distinct, so this may be content rather than label; the two cannot be separated in this design. How much of the hierarchy — none, the category, or the competency unit — a judge should see is a design variable, and we leave its manipulation to future work, where it can be tested on human and model judges alike.

Every aggregate reproduces to within a point. On ESCO, where precision is exact, the two encoders agree on cumulative precision and recall at all three matched thresholds to within 0.5 points, on the band precisions to within 0.7 points, and on the size gradient of transitive precision; the largest precision drop across twenty rank bands falls for both models between the .90- and .85-equivalent ranks. On NCS, matched recovery differs by 0.1–0.4 points, the attitude skew is unchanged (38.7% vs. 38.2%), and the chaining component at .85-equivalent is 29.0% vs. 29.9%. The knee and the failure modes are thus properties of the corpus–task pair; only the number that names the knee is model-specific (.90 for bge-m3, .958 for e5-large), so the recommendation of §4.6 should be read as "the precision knee, located per encoder" rather than as a constant.

The agreement is at the level of aggregates, not of pairs: at the .90-equivalent cut the two encoders select the same pairs only 73% of the time on ESCO and 50% on NCS. This disagreement is exploitable. On ESCO, pairs selected by both encoders at the .85-equivalent cut are 83.1% precise against 76% for either alone, while pairs selected by only one are 42–45% precise — coin flips; at the .90-equivalent cut the intersection reaches 95.1% (vs. 92.8%) at the cost of 15% of the pairs. On the NCS validated pairs the same pattern holds (72.7% strict for pairs above the cut in both encoders, 50–68% for pairs above it in one). Requiring two-encoder agreement is therefore a cheap precision lever for the alignment stage, most valuable in the .85–.90 band where single-encoder precision is lowest.

NCS: the collapse is a weighting artifact, and the cap is not what governs similarity. Under unweighted Jaccard, accepting every community makes agreement with the classification worse than using no embedding at all: taxonomy recovery collapses to four communities (NMI .17 against .30 for rules) and P@5 falls back to the rule level.
